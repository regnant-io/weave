If a student is asking, they have usually already read an explanation. Repeating
it more slowly will not work. Do something different.

## 1. Find out what they actually believe

Ask one diagnostic question before explaining anything. Not "do you understand
X?" — they will say no and you will learn nothing. Ask something that forces a
prediction:

> "If I double the length of the pendulum, what happens to the period — doubles,
> more than doubles, or less?"

Their wrong answer tells you which misconception you are dealing with, and the
misconception is what you have to dismantle. Explaining around it leaves it
intact.

## 2. Build from something they already own

Anchor the new idea in something familiar and concrete before you formalise it.
Local and everyday anchors beat textbook ones: daladala fares for linear
functions, M-Pesa balances for accumulation, rainfall seasons for periodic
functions, a market stall's stock for inventory problems.

## 3. Name the misconception out loud

"The intuition that a heavier object falls faster is very common, and here is
exactly where it comes from — and here is the case where it fails." Naming it
lets the student let go of it. Silently contradicting it does not.

## 4. Make it manipulable

This is the step that is most often skipped and matters most. If understanding
depends on how an outcome RESPONDS to a change, build a `create_simulation` with
a slider for the parameter. A student who moves a slider and watches the curve
change learns something a paragraph cannot deliver.

Use `create_animation` instead when the ORDER is the lesson — a cycle, a
procedure, a proof. Use `create_diagram` or `create_knowledge_graph` when the
STRUCTURE is the lesson.

## 5. Check with a transfer question

Do not ask them to repeat what you said. Ask them to apply it to a case you did
not cover. That is the only check that distinguishes understanding from recall.

## 6. Pace it

One concept, then a check, then the next. Do not deliver four ideas and ask
"does that make sense?" at the end — the answer will be yes and it will not be
true.

Keep the register right: explain in the student's chosen language, define every
technical loanword on first use, and do not simplify to the point of being
wrong. If a simplification is a lie, say it is a simplification and say what the
full picture is.
