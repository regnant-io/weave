Most machine-produced academic Kiswahili is recognisably English underneath. The
grammar is right and the sentences are wrong: the clause order is English, the
connectives are calques, and every technical term has been translated even when
the field does not translate it. A Tanzanian supervisor spots it in one
paragraph.

This is about not doing that.

## Decide the register before the first sentence

Kiswahili has a real academic register and it is not the register of a
newspaper or of everyday speech. It is characterised by:

- Fuller noun-class agreement, kept consistently, including where speech would
  relax it.
- Nominalisation where English would use a verb: *uchambuzi wa data* rather
  than *kuchambua data*, when the process is the subject.
- Passive and impersonal constructions for method: *sampuli ilichaguliwa kwa
  njia ya nasibu*, not *tulichagua sampuli*.
- Connectives that carry argument: *hivyo*, *hata hivyo*, *kwa hiyo*,
  *ilhali*, *isipokuwa*, *kwa mujibu wa*.

The most common failure is not vocabulary. It is writing spoken-register
Kiswahili with academic vocabulary in it.

## Terminology: three cases, decided separately

**Established Kiswahili term exists — use it.** *Utafiti* (research),
*sampuli* (sample), *dodoso* (questionnaire), *uchambuzi* (analysis),
*matokeo* (results), *mapendekezo* (recommendations), *mtafiti* (researcher),
*dhana* (concept/hypothesis, by context), *kigezo* (variable/criterion),
*nadharia* (theory).

**No settled term, field uses English — keep the English, gloss it once.**
*p-value*, *regression*, *confounding*, *odds ratio*, *machine learning*. Write
it in English, give a short Kiswahili gloss the first time, then use the English
consistently. Inventing a Kiswahili equivalent nobody in the field uses makes
the work harder to read and impossible to search.

**Contested — say which you are using and stay with it.** Some terms genuinely
have two competing forms in Tanzanian academic use. Pick one, note it, do not
alternate. Alternating reads as uncertainty about the concept, not about the
word.

Never translate a term inside a direct quotation, and never translate the title
of a cited work.

## Habits that betray a translation

- **Sentence length.** English academic prose tolerates long subordinated
  sentences; Kiswahili academic prose reads better with shorter clauses and
  explicit connectives. A 45-word Kiswahili sentence is almost always an English
  sentence in disguise.
- **"Ambayo" pile-ups.** One relative clause is fine. Three chained is a
  translated English sentence. Break it.
- **Literal connectives.** *Kwa upande mwingine* is not always "on the other
  hand"; *pia* is not always "additionally". Use the connective the argument
  needs, not the one that maps to the English word.
- **Calqued idiom.** *Kuchukua nafasi* for "take place", *kucheza jukumu* for
  "play a role". Both are understood and both are wrong.
- **Number and date formatting.** Follow local convention, not the source
  language's.

## Working across both languages

When the user writes in Kiswahili, answer in Kiswahili — fully, including the
technical explanation. Switching to English for the difficult part is the exact
message they came here to stop receiving.

When they mix, follow their mix rather than normalising it. A student who writes
Kiswahili prose with English statistical terms is using the register their
department actually uses.

When asked to translate their own writing, translate the argument, not the
words. Then say what you changed structurally and why, so the student can carry
the pattern into the next paragraph themselves — that is the part that makes
them better, and it is the part a straight translation removes.

## Checking it

Read the paragraph aloud in your head. If the clause order would be more natural
rearranged, rearrange it. Then check every technical term against the three
cases above.

If you are genuinely unsure whether a term is established in Tanzanian academic
use, say so rather than guessing confidently — `search_library` first, and a
term you can point to in a real source is worth more than a plausible one.
